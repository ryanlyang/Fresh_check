# Learned Feature Repair Before Particle Transformer

## Executive assessment

The short answer is yes: strategies very close in spirit to `lc_mlp_delta` have been tried in several other domains, and they often work surprisingly well when the backbone is already strong. The closest cross-domain analogue I found is not in vision or NLP, but in tabular foundation models: **TFM-Retouche** adds a **near-identity residual input adapter** in front of a frozen strong model, learns a small correction in input space, and reports that this was the **only** adaptation strategy among its tested options that reliably beat the frozen base. In the same head-to-head, LoRA and full fine-tuning did not improve the strong base, while a non-residual “replace the input” encoder hurt performance. That is extremely close to your emerging story that a small, learned, bounded feature correction can outperform fancier architectural changes when the downstream transformer is already very capable. citeturn22view0turn27view0

Across domains, the pattern is consistent. In vision, **Visual Prompt Tuning** improves a frozen ViT by modifying the **input sequence** rather than the backbone and beats full fine-tuning on **20 of 24** downstream tasks while tuning **less than 1%** of parameters. In NLP, **prefix tuning** and **P-Tuning v2** show that small learned input-space or layer-input modifications can match or beat full fine-tuning in some regimes with only **0.1% to 3%** trainable parameters. In audio, **LEAF** and **SincNet** replace fixed audio front ends with lightweight learned ones and improve downstream recognition. In vision under degradation, **task-driven super-resolution** improves a detector more than standard perceptual restoration does, showing that the best “repair” for a downstream model is often **task-aware**, not merely physically plausible. citeturn16view0turn16view2turn12view8turn12view9turn14view0turn15view6turn13view1

So the most likely interpretation of your result is not “the MLP just adds some extra parameters.” The literature points to a more specific explanation: a strong backbone can still be bottlenecked by the **representation it receives at its interface**, especially under degradation, domain shift, noisy feature engineering, or suboptimal tokenization. A small residual adapter can improve that interface while preserving almost all of the pretrained model’s useful structure. That is exactly the regime where near-identity, feature-level residual correction tends to shine. citeturn22view0turn21view0turn18view3turn12view4

## Evidence from adjacent literatures

The **strongest direct analogue** is the recent tabular work **TFM-Retouche**. It introduces a dimension-preserving, near-identity residual adapter before a frozen tabular foundation model, with per-feature gates and an explicit “identity guard” that falls back to the unmodified model when adaptation does not help on validation. The paper argues that the issue for already-strong pretrained models is no longer raw capacity but **alignment** between the fixed inductive biases of the backbone and the specifics of the downstream dataset. In its head-to-head comparison, Retouche is reported as the **only adaptation strategy that beats the unmodified base**; LoRA and full supervised fine-tuning do not produce measurable gains over the strong frozen backbone, and replacing the input with a learned encoder hurts. That is about as close as the current literature gets to your `F' = F + delta(F)` hypothesis. citeturn22view0turn27view0

Vision provides two complementary analogies. First, **Visual Prompt Tuning** shows that changing the input sequence seen by the transformer can outperform changing the backbone itself: VPT beats other transfer baselines and surpasses full fine-tuning on **20 out of 24** tasks with less than **1%** task-specific parameters. Second, in degraded-image settings, **Task-Driven Super-Resolution** shows that front-end restoration trained jointly for the downstream task can strongly outperform ordinary restoration. On VOC2007, for example, the task-driven front end improved detector mAP from **53.6 to 62.2** at 4x enlargement and from **22.9 to 37.5** at 8x enlargement versus the best SR fine-tuning baseline, while also outperforming standard SR methods under blur and noise. The important lesson is that the best repair layer is often not the one that makes inputs “look best” to humans, but the one that makes them **most useful to the downstream recognizer**. citeturn16view0turn16view2turn13view1turn13view2

Your original “local compression inside particles” idea also has real precedent, but those precedents address a somewhat different bottleneck. **Transformer in Transformer** models “words inside patches” and reports about **1.7%** higher ImageNet top-1 than a comparable ViT at similar compute, while **Tokens-to-Token ViT** argues that naive tokenization misses local structure and reports **more than 3 percentage points** improvement over vanilla ViT when trained from scratch on ImageNet. In NLP, **CANINE** uses character-level inputs plus learned downsampling before the transformer and beats mBERT by **2.8 F1** on TyDi QA with fewer parameters, while **MEGABYTE** uses local-within-patch and global-between-patch modeling to make long-sequence byte models competitive. These papers justify your local-compression direction, but they mostly argue that local structure helps when the **tokenization itself is crude or too expensive**, not necessarily that local compression is the best way to adapt an already-strong backbone on a fixed downstream task. citeturn8search1turn23search0turn12view7turn11view6

NLP parameter-efficient tuning papers reinforce the idea that input-space interventions can be far more powerful than their size suggests. **Prefix-Tuning** learns a continuous prefix that the frozen LM attends to and achieves performance comparable to fine-tuning while training only **0.1%** of parameters, with advantages in low-data and unseen-topic regimes. **P-Tuning v2** extends that idea to deep prompts at every layer input and reports that it can match fine-tuning across a wider range of NLU tasks with only **0.1% to 3%** trainable parameters. These are not “feature repair” in the detector-sense, but they are strong evidence that modifying what a transformer sees at its interface can redirect a large pretrained model very effectively, sometimes more effectively than classifier-head-only adaptation. citeturn12view8turn12view9

Audio and speech are especially relevant because they show success for learned front ends that replace fixed handcrafted preprocessing. **LEAF** learns filtering, pooling, compression, and normalization end-to-end, works as a drop-in replacement for mel filterbanks, and reports consistent improvements across **eight** diverse audio tasks at negligible parameter cost. **SincNet** learns a structured first layer directly from raw waveform and reports lower classification error than CNN baselines, for example **1.00 to 0.96** CER on LibriSpeech speaker identification and **0.58 to 0.51** EER on speaker verification. The key analogy is that once the backbone is strong, learned improvements to the **measurement-to-representation interface** can still matter. citeturn14view0turn12view1turn15view6turn15view7

HEP and scientific ML already use related ideas, although usually framed as calibration or reconstruction rather than “input adapters.” A CMS jet-energy-correction study used high-dimensional jet features and improved nominal jet resolution by **upward of 15%**. LHCb’s 2026 jet-reconstruction paper presents an ML regressor for jet-energy calibration and a DNN tagger, explicitly framing the regressor as an upstream correction to improve downstream physics sensitivity. More generally, recent scientific-ML work on sensor drift uses a learnable calibration transformation to undo detector aging effects by matching distributions. So while I did not find a canonical particle-tagging paper that is identical to `lc_mlp_delta`, the HEP literature is already comfortable with the broader idea that **learned upstream correction layers** can materially improve downstream inference. citeturn4view3turn28view0turn18view4

## Why a residual input adapter can beat a strong backbone

The most convincing mechanism is **task-aware repair under shift**. HLT degradation is an explicit shift between the feature statistics the model would ideally like and the ones it actually receives. That is the setting where simple adaptation layers often work best. **AdaBN** argues that label-related knowledge can remain in the weights while domain-related information is reflected in feature statistics, so changing normalization or calibration at the interface can recover transferability without rewriting the whole model. **REFINE** goes even further and proves that augmenting pretrained features with a trainable residual target-side encoder can avoid negative transfer under mild conditions, while improving robustness under distribution shift, label noise, semantic perturbation, and class imbalance. In your case, an HLT residual adapter can be read as a particle-wise domain-alignment map that corrects HLT-specific distortions before ParT does the harder relational reasoning. citeturn18view3turn21view0

The second mechanism is **better feature parameterization or geometry**. Several literatures argue that the choice of input representation is an underexplored performance lever even when the backbone is unchanged. In tabular DL, Gorishniy et al. show that embeddings for numerical features can produce **significant performance boosts** and help many backbones, not just transformers; in some cases, better input embeddings let simple MLP-like models match attention-based architectures. **TabTransformer** likewise shows that contextualized feature embeddings improve mean AUC and robustness to missing and noisy features. If ParT is already excellent at reasoning over particle tokens and pairwise interactions, then an adapter that merely places the particle features into a more “attention-friendly” local geometry could give you gains without changing ParT’s high-level reasoning at all. citeturn12view4turn12view5turn12view6turn26view0

The third mechanism is **preserving pretrained structure while adding only the missing residual**. This is the same high-level argument behind side networks, residual learning, and prompt tuning. **Side-Tuning** explicitly frames adaptation as learning a small additive correction on top of a frozen base representation and reports that it can match or slightly beat fine-tuning in some settings while avoiding forgetting and using fewer trainable parameters. **Visual Prompt Tuning** and **Prefix-Tuning** show the same structural principle in transformers: do not destroy the pretrained model’s useful internal circuit; instead, perturb the interface enough that the existing circuit becomes useful for the new target. That is a strong reason to prefer a near-identity adapter to a fully replacing front end or an aggressive re-embedding scheme. citeturn24view0turn24view1turn16view0turn12view8

The fourth mechanism is **regularization through the identity prior**. A bounded or gated residual adapter is heavily biased toward “do nothing unless the gradient consistently says otherwise.” That bias is often useful when the backbone already performs well and only a subset of channels need adjustment. TFM-Retouche makes this explicit with per-channel gates initialized near zero and an identity guard on validation, and it reports that the identity guard and trainable gated residual are jointly load-bearing. This is exactly the kind of structure I would want in HLT feature repair: not an unconstrained preprocessing block that can arbitrarily rewrite PF inputs, but a conservative, dimension-preserving adapter that can only move features where there is strong evidence that doing so helps. citeturn22view0turn27view0

Could the gain still be “just extra capacity”? Yes, in principle. But several of the strongest analogies cut against that being the whole story. In TFM-Retouche, **weight-space** adaptation with LoRA or full supervised fine-tuning did not beat the frozen base, whereas the **input-space residual nudge** did. In the same study, replacing the input was worse than nudging it. In task-driven SR, higher-PSNR restoration was not the best detector front end. These are exactly the kinds of results you get when the improvement comes from a **task-specific interface correction**, not from generic added capacity. citeturn27view0turn13view1

## Implications for HLT and Offline ParT

For **HLT ParT**, the literature makes your result highly plausible. HLT degradation gives you an obvious source of systematic distortion, and the best-matching external examples are all cases where a strong downstream model benefited from a small learned front end that aligned the input distribution to the backbone’s implicit expectations. If your adapter is bounded, per-particle, and end-to-end optimized for tagging, it is very plausible that it is learning a mixture of calibration, denoising, feature scaling, and mild nonlinear reparameterization that makes ParT’s existing attention heads more useful. In other words, HLT ParT may already know how to use the “right” token geometry, and `lc_mlp_delta` may simply be making HLT particles look more like that geometry. citeturn18view3turn21view0turn27view0

For **Offline ParT**, I think improvement is also plausible, but the expected gain is smaller and the burden of proof is higher. The key literature point is that even in domains without obvious sensor degradation, learned input adaptation can still help because the issue is not only corruption but also **representation mismatch**. Numerical embedding papers, VPT, and tabular residual adapters all show that strong backbones can still be bottlenecked by their interface. The TFM-Retouche result is especially relevant here because it argues that, once basic capacity is no longer the bottleneck, the remaining gap is often one of **alignment** between a generic pretrained prior and the specifics of a task. By analogy, Offline PF features may already be “good,” but not necessarily the optimal coordinates for the downstream Hgg versus QCD decision boundary. citeturn12view4turn16view0turn22view0

That said, the literature also tells you what would make Offline gains believable. They would be more convincing if the learned deltas are small, structured, and stable across seeds; if the same-parameter larger ParT does **not** recover the gain; if a post-embedding adapter underperforms a raw-feature adapter; and if the feature changes line up with physically interpretable nuisance channels such as kinematics, PID-like channels, or known reconstruction pathologies. If the deltas are large, unstable, or concentrated in arbitrary channels, then the “repair” story weakens and the “extra optimization/capacity” story gets stronger. The fact that strong ParT variants can still improve on the original architecture supports the idea that there is still headroom, but it does not by itself prove that an input adapter is the correct route. citeturn20search5turn27view0turn26view0

My overall judgment is therefore: **pivot partway, not all the way**. You should treat “Learned HLT/Offline Feature Repair Adapter before Particle Transformer” as a serious primary line, because the external literature supports it unusually well. But I would not yet discard local compression entirely. The local-compression family is best understood as a different hypothesis: that the particle tokenization itself is too crude and needs intra-particle structure. The current evidence you described suggests that, for this task and this degradation level, the dominant bottleneck may instead be **feature alignment before token reasoning**. That means the repair-adapter line should become the default hypothesis, and local compression should become a targeted secondary ablation rather than the main story for now. citeturn23search0turn8search1turn11view6turn27view0

## Controls that can falsify the feature-repair story

I would structure the next round of experiments so that each control cleanly discriminates between **real feature repair**, **generic extra capacity**, and **warm-start optimization effects**.

- **Frozen ParT, adapter-only** versus **full fine-tuning without adapter** is the first decisive split. If adapter-only already beats the frozen baseline, that is strong evidence that the interface is a bottleneck. If full fine-tuning without adapter matches the gain, the improvement may be mostly from extra optimization freedom. The fact that TFM-Retouche’s input residual beat weight-space tuning on a strong frozen base makes this a particularly important control. citeturn27view0

- **Same-parameter larger ParT** and **same-parameter post-embedding adapter** are the best “extra capacity” tests. If a raw-feature residual adapter wins over both, then the gain is likely about repairing the physics-facing interface rather than simply giving the network more parameters somewhere. TFM-Retouche is again useful here because it found that a residual input nudge beat weight-space adaptation, and that replacing the input hurt when the backbone was already strong. citeturn27view0

- **Residual nudge versus input replacement** should be an explicit ablation. Compare `F' = F + delta(F)` to a same-size encoder that outputs a fresh `F_tilde`. The tabular evidence says this matters: a dimension-preserving residual nudge can win where a replacement encoder loses, precisely because the strong backbone already knows how to use the original representation and only needs small corrections. citeturn22view0turn27view0

- **Random, shuffled, and label-shuffled controls** should attack the semantic content of the learned delta. Shuffle delta vectors across particles within an event, across events within a batch, and across features; train the adapter with shuffled labels; and test a random frozen adapter with the same gate statistics. If the gain disappears under these manipulations, that strongly supports meaningful task-aware repair rather than generic stochastic regularization.

- **Teacher and distillation controls** would directly probe the “HLT-to-Offline repair” hypothesis. Train the adapter to match an **Offline ParT** teacher’s logits, a mid-layer particle embedding, or both, then compare to pure supervised tagging. If the distilled adapter improves HLT ParT and especially if its learned deltas become more structured, that is strong evidence that the adapter is learning a semantically meaningful correction toward a higher-fidelity representation rather than merely exploiting label leakage.

- **Feature-wise delta diagnostics** should become a first-class result, not an afterthought. Plot per-feature delta means, variances, sign asymmetries, correlations with original features, correlations with particle type and pT bins, and agreement across seeds. If the adapter really repairs HLT features, you should see consistent and interpretable patterns rather than diffuse unstable deltas. LEAF, SincNet, and AdaBN are useful precedents here because their claimed successes are partly supported by the interpretability of learned front-end corrections or statistics. citeturn14view0turn15view6turn18view3

- **Multiple seeds, locked validation protocol, and final threshold chosen only on validation** are mandatory. Input-space adaptation methods can have nontrivial variance, and your selection rule is itself part of the scientific claim. I would report seed means and standard deviations for the baseline, adapter, larger-ParT control, and post-embedding control on the exact same split and with the same validation rule.

- **Paired HLT-Off line supervision, if available**, is the cleanest validation of the repair story. If you have event-level or particle-level pairing, add an auxiliary loss that predicts Offline features or embeddings from HLT features. Then check whether the supervised tagging gain and the HLT→Offline reconstruction gain rise together. If they do, that is powerful evidence for genuine feature repair.

If you run those controls and the gain survives, I think you will have a very strong case that `lc_mlp_delta` is not a cheap artifact but a meaningful front-end improvement. If the gain collapses under those controls, then the honest conclusion will be that the current benefit came mostly from optimization freedom or selection noise, and that is still scientifically useful to know.

## Best next architecture to try

The literature suggests that the strongest next move is **not** a larger local-compression transformer. It is a more deliberate version of `lc_mlp_delta`: a **gated, near-identity, per-particle feature repair adapter** that is explicitly designed to preserve the original PF representation while allowing structured corrections where needed. The closest blueprint comes from TFM-Retouche’s per-channel gated residual nudge, combined with the learned normalization and compression lessons from LEAF and the domain-statistics insight from AdaBN. citeturn22view0turn27view0turn14view0turn18view3

Concretely, I would try a front end with three components inside each particle’s repair block. First, a **feature-wise calibration branch** that learns affine scale and shift, ideally conditioned on particle type or mask status. Second, a **cross-feature interaction branch**, either a tiny residual MLP or a DCNv2-style cross module, because the tabular literature shows that explicit low-order feature interactions often matter at the interface. Third, a **bounded per-feature gate** initialized near zero, so the network starts as identity and only moves channels that consistently help on the validation objective. The output should remain `F' = F + α ⊙ Δ(F)`, not a replacement encoding. citeturn22view0turn12view4

If you can support it, I would also add an **auxiliary teacher loss** from an Offline ParT or stronger teacher. For HLT, that could be KL divergence on logits plus an L2 or cosine loss on one intermediate particle embedding layer. This mirrors the logic of task-driven restoration and safe residual adaptation: the adapter should be optimized not only for final discrimination but also for producing an input representation that a stronger downstream model would consider “more canonical.” The distillation target gives the repair layer a more specific direction than labels alone. citeturn13view1turn21view0

One extra idea from TFM-Retouche that is unusually appealing for your setting is an **identity guard**. After training, compare the adapted path and the identity path on validation and only keep the adapter when it clearly helps. In their tabular results, the identity guard was one of the most important ingredients. In HLT tagging, this would give you a conservative deployment story: the adapter is allowed to help, but it is not allowed to degrade a strong baseline silently. citeturn27view0

So if I had to recommend a single architecture family to prioritize, it would be this:

**Learned Feature Repair Adapter before ParT**  
A near-identity, per-particle, dimension-preserving residual adapter with:
- feature-wise affine calibration,
- a tiny cross-feature residual block,
- per-feature bounded gates initialized near zero,
- optional Offline-teacher distillation on logits and/or particle embeddings,
- and a validation-based identity guard.

That is the architecture most strongly suggested by the literature you asked me to sweep. The broader conclusion is that your surprising control result is not an anomaly. It fits a real cross-domain pattern: when a transformer backbone is already strong, the biggest remaining win is often not replacing it, but **repairing what it sees**. citeturn22view0turn27view0turn16view0turn12view8turn14view0turn18view3turn21view0
